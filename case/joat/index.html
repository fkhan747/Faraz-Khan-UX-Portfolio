Case study · Crowdfunding commerce

Threadfold

Selling a custom tee meant six tools, a printer with minimums, and your own money on the line. We built the thing that replaced all of it, starting from a blank page and a risky assumption.

My part in it

From a blank page to a live platform

What I owned

  • Senior UX and UI designer, from discovery to launch
  • The Design Studio, where a tee gets made
  • The three-step campaign flow and the public storefront
  • The raiser back office: orders, payouts, account

Shape of the work

  • Responsive web, desktop and mobile
  • 12 desktop screens, 4 mobile layouts
  • Assumption testing and research before any interface
  • Built from nothing: no existing product, no existing users

How a campaign works

You publicise. We produce. You keep the profit.

Almost nobody arriving on this site had seen a crowdfunded t-shirt before, so the whole model had to land in one scroll before anyone would sign up. Here it is in the three steps the homepage used.

01

Design it in minutes

Pick a product and a colour, drop in artwork, text or an upload, and watch it appear on the tee. A base cost shows up beside the canvas while you work.

02

Set a price and promote it

Choose a sales goal, an end date and a per-tee price. The flow shows the minimum profit before launch. Then share the link and push traffic at it.

03

We produce, you profit

Hit the goal and Threadfold prints, ships to every buyer and transfers the profit. Miss it and nobody pays anything. No inventory, no upfront cost, no risk to the creator.

The stack it replaced

What selling a tee used to take

To sell a custom tee you had to be your own production manager: design somewhere, mock it up somewhere else, find a printer with a minimum order, take payments yourself, and arrange the shipping. Every handoff added cost and risk before a single shirt sold.

  • A stack of disconnected tools. Design in one place, mock up in another, print with a vendor, collect payments somewhere else, ship it yourself. Nothing talked to anything.
  • Money on the line first. High minimum orders and pay-before-print meant real cash committed before any demand was proven.
  • Nobody owned the journey. One vendor falling over stalled the whole campaign, and the creator carried it alone.
  • Cold traffic only. Campaigns lived or died on a link dropped into a Facebook ad, so the funnel had to convert strangers.

The bet

One platform owns everything, so the creator only owns the idea.

  • If design, pricing, payments, production, shipping and payout all sit in one place, the creator has nothing left to wrangle.
  • And if nothing is printed until the campaign funds, the upfront cost goes to zero and the risk moves off the creator entirely.
  • That is the whole product in one sentence: you publicise, we produce, you keep the profit.
  • My job was to make a first-time visitor believe that in one scroll, then walk them through it without a handoff.

What could kill it

I did not start with screens. I started with what could kill this.

  • The model only works if a chain of beliefs all hold, and most of them had no evidence behind them at all. So the first job was writing them down and sorting them by what happens if they are wrong.
  • Importance on one axis, evidence on the other. Everything in the top-left corner is a bet the business dies on, with nothing supporting it yet.
  • Three landed there. Creators will promote their own campaign. Buyers will pay for a shirt that does not exist yet. Buyers will trust an unknown site with their card.
  • The first one is the whole company. Threadfold has no audience of its own: every sale comes through a link the creator shares. If creators do not promote, nothing else on this page matters.
  • So that became the thing to design for, and the thing to test first, before a single interface decision got made.
How it was run

A two-hour workshop with the two founders and the engineer. Everyone wrote beliefs on cards alone first, so the loudest person in the room did not set the list, and we ended up with 31 of them. Placing each card was the argument: importance was quick to agree on, evidence was not, and every time somebody said "we know that" I asked how we knew. Most of those cards moved left. The three that ended up top-left got a test each before I opened a design tool.

Studying both sides

Two user groups, and no way to study them the same way.

  • Creators were few, findable and generous with time. Buyers were many, anonymous, and gone in ninety seconds. Running the same method on both would have meant doing one of them badly.
  • So creators got depth: nine contextual inquiry sessions in their own rooms and hostels, watching them try to make a design with the tools they already had. Four kept a diary through a live campaign.
  • Buyers got breadth: 340 intercept survey responses off live campaigns, plus five-second tests on the campaign page to find out what a stranger takes away before they decide to scroll.
  • I also made a sequencing call here. Roughly two-thirds of failed marketplaces die on the supply side, not demand, so creators got the deep research and the first release. Buyers got a page that converts and not much else.
How it was run

Creators were recruited through college notice boards and two student bands, and I went to them rather than making them come to us, because the point was to see the laptop and the half-finished poster on it. Sessions ran about an hour, task-based, watching them try to make a design with whatever they already used. Buyers were intercepted on live campaign pages with a three-question exit survey, which is the only honest way to reach someone who was never going to sit down with me.

What came back

Four things I did not expect.

One of them broke the assumption the whole model rested on, which was the point of testing it before designing anything.

Nobody could picture the finished thing

Every creator I sat with described the same block: they could imagine the design and they could imagine wearing it, and nothing in between. Sketching on paper did not help because paper is not a t-shirt. This is why the live preview is the centre of the studio and not a step at the end.

The money question came first, not last

I expected creativity questions. I got "what do I make on this?" in the first five minutes of seven out of nine sessions. Pricing was not a boring form to get through. It was the thing they were actually deciding.

"I do not want to ask my friends for money"

The riskiest assumption cracked here. Creators would happily share something they had made; they would not run a fundraiser. So the product had to feel like selling a product, not soliciting donations, which changed the language on every screen.

Buyers were not backing a cause, they were buying a shirt

The survey was blunt about it. Most buyers described the purchase as buying a tee from someone they knew. The crowdfunding mechanic mattered to them only as a reason it might not arrive.

How it was run

Two of us affinity-mapped the session notes independently before comparing, which is slower but stops one person's pet theory becoming the finding. Anything only one of us had seen got flagged rather than dropped, and the buyer survey was coded separately so a strong creator quote could not colour how the buyer data read.

Service blueprint

A journey map would have shown me half the product.

  • The promise here is "we produce, you profit". Everything in the second half of that sentence, printing, quality checks, packing, courier, payment capture, payout, is invisible to both users.
  • A journey map captures what the two people do and feel. It cannot show you the thing that makes or breaks this service, which is what the business does underneath them.
  • So I blueprinted it: physical evidence, creator actions, buyer actions, then the line of visibility, then everything backstage and the systems supporting it.
  • Two things fell out of it immediately. The creator has nothing to do between the goal being hit and the money arriving, which is a silence long enough to lose trust in. And payment is held rather than captured until the goal is met, which is the mechanic the no-risk promise is actually made of, and nowhere in the UI said so.
  • Both of those became interface work: a status the creator can watch during the silent stretch, and plain language about the held payment next to the buy button.
How it was run

I walked one real order end to end and wrote down every step, including the ones that happened in a WhatsApp group rather than in software. Then I sat with operations and the print partner and mapped it on a wall in lanes, working downward: what the two users see, then what we do that they never see, then the systems underneath. Both problems it surfaced were gaps in the lanes rather than on the screens, which is exactly why a journey map would have missed them.

Two sides, one page

Two journeys that only meet at the campaign page.

  • The raiser designs, prices and launches. The buyer arrives from an ad or a shared link and orders.
  • Neither should carry the other's weight, so I split the flows and let them meet at exactly one place.
  • Everything between the two, production, payments and shipping, is the platform's job and stays off both screens.

Under one roof

A creator tool, a shop and a back office under one roof.

  • The hard part of the architecture was that none of the three could feel bolted on to the other two.
  • So I organised it around three jobs rather than three products: create a campaign, back a campaign, manage what you launched.
  • That is why going live never feels like switching apps. It is the same vocabulary either side of the launch button.

Testing the structure

The first structure was ours, not theirs.

  • I had grouped the navigation the way we talked about the product internally, which is the most common way to get this wrong.
  • 48 participants sorted 34 content cards. What came back grouped around what people were trying to do rather than which part of the company owned it.
  • Tree testing the original structure gave 52% task success, well under the 75% that counts as a working navigation. Regrouped around three jobs, create, back, manage, it went to 81%.
  • First-click testing then checked the visual implementation, moving correct first clicks from 44% to 78%. That matters more than it sounds: a correct first click makes someone roughly three times more likely to finish the task at all.
How it was run

An open card sort, run remotely so people grouped and named things without me in the room nudging the vocabulary. I then rebuilt the resulting tree as a bare text hierarchy with no visual design on it at all and set real tasks against it, so the only thing under test was whether the labels and grouping made sense. First-click testing came last, on actual screens, because that measures the visual implementation rather than the structure.

Graybox first

Three screens carried the weight, so those got options.

I blocked out the heaviest layouts in grayscale and pressure-tested each against its one job, before any visual design could make a weak structure look convincing.

The Design Studio

Nothing to install, and the file never leaves the platform.

  • The old way started by opening design software and ended by emailing a printer. This starts and ends in the browser.
  • Pick a product and colour, then add artwork, text, an upload or a background, with the tee updating live in the middle of the screen.
  • Front and back, real product colours, and a curated artwork library, so a blank tee becomes something sellable fast.
  • A base cost sits beside the canvas while you design. The economics start before the campaign flow even opens.

Where the money shows up

Most platforms hide the economics. I put them on the screen.

  • Pricing is the step where a first-time creator decides whether this is worth doing, so burying it in a form was never going to work.
  • A goal slider, an end date and a per-tee price drive a live minimum-profit figure that moves as you move them.
  • The reassurance that actually matters, that you pay nothing if the campaign falls short, sits next to the launch button rather than in a help page.
  • Three steps total: design the tee, set the price, add the description. Then it is live and shareable.

After it goes live

The back office speaks the same language as the studio.

  • Once a campaign is running the raiser needs four things: track orders, watch the goal, manage the account, and get paid.
  • I kept all of it in the same calm single-focus layout as the studio, so launching never feels like being handed to a different product.
  • The payout view is the moment the model pays off for the creator, so it is the plainest screen in the set. No cleverness anywhere near someone's money.

Desktop down to phone

Most of the traffic arrived on a phone.

  • Campaigns were promoted through Facebook ads and shared links, which meant the majority of first impressions happened on a small screen.
  • So the studio and the storefront both scale from a wide desktop canvas down to a single column.
  • On mobile the model collapses to the same three promises in one scroll: design in minutes, set your price, we produce and you profit.
  • This is also the thing I would do differently. We designed desktop-first and adapted down. The traffic said we had it backwards.

The rest of the product

Both sides of the platform.

Five rounds

Each round took something away.

Five participants per side per round, which is where new findings dry up for a single group. Two distinct groups meant ten people a round, not five.

01

Round one killed the wizard

The first studio was a step-by-step wizard: pick product, then colour, then artwork, then text. Creators hated being told the order. Four of five backed out at the colour step to go and find artwork first. The studio became a single canvas with a controls rail, and nothing enforcing sequence.

02

Round two moved the money

Pricing sat on its own screen, after the design. Creators reached the price step, discovered their margin was thin, and went back to change the design, which meant losing work. The base cost moved into the studio, live, next to the canvas.

03

Round three rewrote the promise

"No risk" tested as marketing noise. People did not believe it and could not say what it meant. Rewritten as the mechanic itself, that nobody is charged unless the goal is met, it stopped being a claim and started being an explanation.

04

Round four was the buyer's turn

With creators settled I ran the buyer side properly. Forced account creation before checkout lost people outright, which matches the ecommerce research: it is the second most common reason carts get abandoned, at 24%. Guest checkout went in and the field count came down from 21 to 13.

05

Round five found nothing new

That was the signal to stop. Five participants per side per round, which is the sample where new findings dry up for a homogeneous group, and with two distinct groups that meant ten people a round rather than five.

How it was run

Moderated and task-based, in person for creators and remote for buyers. I gave people a goal rather than a script ("get to a design you would put your name on") and then stayed quiet, timing where they stalled. Each round changed one structural thing, so the next round tested that change rather than a new product. The round-five stop rule was written before round one, so ending it was a decision made in advance rather than when I got tired.

On the record

What was written about it at the time.

  • One of the first crowdfunding platforms for t-shirts and custom apparel in India.
  • Covered by national newspapers and the technology and startup press.
  • Used for college tees, fan groups, artwork and social causes, including campaigns run with national children's charities.
  • Reported by press to have grown quickly through 2014 and 2015.

With hindsight

Four things I would do differently, and one I would keep.

  • The studio offered too much at once. I would stage the controls harder and get a raiser to a sellable design in three taps, revealing the power later.
  • We designed desktop-first and the traffic was mobile. I would lead with the phone campaign flow rather than retrofitting it.
  • The economics screen was never tested with real raisers. It was the strongest idea in the product and the least validated, which is a bad combination.
  • Campaign pages needed to be landing pages. Converting cold ad traffic is its own discipline and a one-size template was not it.
  • What I would keep: putting the money on the screen. Every instinct in commerce design says hide the pricing mechanics. Surfacing them is what made the model believable.
